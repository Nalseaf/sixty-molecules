Sixty Molecules

358 people gave blood. Sixty molecules gave the game away.

A lab measured 758 different molecules in each person's plasma. About half of the people had type 2 diabetes. The question: which molecules can tell the two groups apart?

Chapter 1 · The clean-up

First, decide which molecules to trust

The machine can't find every molecule in every person. Some are simply too rare. If a molecule is missing in most people, any comparison built on it is shaky, so it has to go.

Notice who gets cut first: the things only some people have in their blood. Painkillers, a sweetener, a marker of smoking. Caffeine never leaves. It was found in every single sample.

Chapter 2 · The trap

Compare the groups, and 155 molecules light up

That looks like a huge result. It is partly an illusion. In this study, people with diabetes were on average 53 years old and people without were 40. Blood changes with age. Some of those 155 are telling us who is older.

Chapter 3 · The surprise

Quick guess: which one is the best tell?

Out of the 501 molecules tested, which separates people with diabetes from people without most clearly?

Pick one to see the ranking.

The winner is a sugar most people have never heard of: 1,5-anhydroglucitol. It comes from food and the kidneys normally recycle it back into the blood. When blood sugar runs high, glucose spills into the urine and blocks that recycling, so 1,5-anhydroglucitol drains away.

In this cohort, people with diabetes had about 34% of the level seen in people without. It follows HbA1c, the standard long-term blood sugar test, like a staircase.

Chapter 4 · The fingerprints

Blood also remembers your pharmacy

Remember the molecules that were dropped in Chapter 1 for being too rare? One of them was metformin, the most common diabetes drug. It was rare overall for a simple reason.

This matters for reading the results. When a molecule differs between the groups, it could be the disease. It could also be the treatment. A breakdown product of aspirin is among the sixty, which more likely reflects what people take than the disease itself.
